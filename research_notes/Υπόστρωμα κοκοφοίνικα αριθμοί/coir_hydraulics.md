# Coir (coir pith / coco peat / coir slabs): physical and hydraulic properties, with sources

Compiled 2026-10-27 for approval of crop-model parameters. **Rule followed:** every number below is copied from a source that was actually opened. The "seen in" column says how much of the source was read. Numbers marked **DERIVED** were not published. I calculated them from published values, and the formula is shown, so they can be checked or thrown out. No "typical values" were added.

Abbreviations: TPS = total pore space; AC = air content (air capacity); CC = container capacity; EAW = easily available water (−1 to −5 kPa); WBC = water buffering capacity (−5 to −10 kPa); BD = dry bulk density; θ = volumetric water content (% v/v); −1 kPa = −10 cm H2O = pF 1.

## 7. Container-height effect (slab 7.5–15 cm vs laboratory −1 kPa)

**Theory.** BF87 states three rules. (1) The container bottom acts as a perched water table, so suction is zero at the base. (2) At equilibrium after drainage, suction in cm H2O equals the height above the base. (3) Container water content is therefore the retention curve integrated from 0 to H, using zones of 2 cm or less. Mi89 used zones of 0.5 cm with a van Genuchten curve, and GS07 integrated the curve directly:

  θ_container(H) = (1/H) ∫₀ᴴ θ(h) dh,  AC_container = TPS − θ_container  (GS07, Methods; Mi89 ECV model; BF87, p. 180–181)

For a straight-sided slab this needs only the retention curve θ(h) from 0 to H cm, which is 0 to 1 kPa for a 10 cm slab. The lab value at −1 kPa therefore matches the water content at the **top** surface of a 10 cm slab. The whole-slab average is always wetter and has less air.

**Practical consequence for the model.** If "field capacity" for a 10 cm slab is set to the lab θ(−1 kPa), the model **under-estimates** the water the slab holds after drainage and **over-estimates** its air. The size of the error depends on the slope of the retention curve between 0 and −1 kPa. That slope is small for fine coir and large for coarse or chip-rich coir. The best-supported approach is to integrate a fitted retention curve over the slab height (GS07, Mi89). A measured coir slab (SB22, 14.5 cm) was within ~2 points of the −1 kPa value.

## Conflicts and caveats

1. **The method determines the number.** The two main "schools" differ by up to 10 points on the same kind of material:
   - US NCSU / Byrne & Carty: free drainage in 7.5 cm cores, giving "container capacity". This yields **low TPS (84–90 %) and low air (5–13 %)** (Ev96, Ko99).
   - European De Boodt / EN 13041: suction table at −1 kPa. This yields **TPS 94–98 % and air 24–89 %** (Ab05, Wo24).

   This is the core-height effect (§7) plus differences in saturation and packing. Never mix a TPS from one method with a θ from the other. For "saturation" in the model, Ab05, Wo24, Go20 and SB22 agree on **~87–98 %**. The lower Ev96/Ko99 values (78–91 %) come from the CC-core method.
2. **Coir is not one material.** θ at −1 kPa ranges 9–79 % and θ at −10 kPa ranges 8–51 % across the sources. The coarseness index (fibre/chip vs pith) explains most of the spread (Ab05). A slab parameter set should be tied to a specific product grade, ideally from an EN 13041 analysis of that product (as Wo24 did for Botanicoir).
3. **Woznicki 2024 (Botanicoir) vs Soto-Bravo 2022 ("Fico") show the spread within commercial slab coir.** At −10 kPa the water content is 23.7 % vs ~51 %. EAW is 15.8 vs 23.6 %.
4. **The −10 kPa "floor" is not a wilting point.** SB22 calls θ(10 kPa) "unavailable water", which is a convention, not a measurement of plant water stress. Go20 used −440 cm (−43 kPa) as the dry end for tomato and found θ = 33 % still present there. Ab05 states that the De Boodt method underestimates EAW and WBC for coarse coir relative to what roots can actually take up (p. 2140).
5. **Internal inconsistencies seen:**
   - Ev96 gives the AFP maximum as 12.6 in the abstract and 12.5 in the text and table.
   - Ko99 Table 2 prints BD 0.4–0.6 g/cm³. This is implausible; the text says 0.06–0.07.
   - SB22 gives unavailable water as 49.9, 51.28 and 52.35 % in three places, and its θCC optimum as 55–70 in the table and 50–70 in the text.
   - The GHL manufacturer sheet's 32 % solids and 68 % porosity disagree with every peer-reviewed coir value (solids 2–16 %). Treat it as unreliable.
6. **Derived values** (θ at −1, −5 and −10 kPa from Ab05; all Go20-curve values; the container averages) are my arithmetic on published numbers. They are for checking only and are not citable as published.
7. **Re-use and aging:** no full-text primary source with numbers was found. The direction, from one abstract (Ur08), is less air and more water after one crop. Wo24 has the data in a supplement that was not opened. Shrinkage on drying for virgin coir is 12.6 % (Wo24), which is relevant if slabs dry out between crops.
8. **Ksat** has a single primary value (56 cm/h at BD 0.11; Go20), and Go20 itself reports that literature values differ by more than 10×.
9. **Not accessed** (no numbers taken): De Boodt & Verdonck 1972 (beyond the abstract), Abad et al. 1993, Fonteno 1993, Raviv & Lieth (2008/2019), Fornes et al. 2003, Noguera et al. 2000 and 2003 (beyond abstracts). No Greek coir-slab hydraulic study was found; GS07 (Gizas & Savvas, Arta) is about pumice.
