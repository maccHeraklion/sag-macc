# Root-zone salinity under drip irrigation: depth interpretation, aggregation and decision rules (alerts, leaching)

Scope: how to read soil salinity across depth and the root zone under drip/micro-irrigation; how root-zone salinity is aggregated in standard models; the standard decision rules (Maas–Hoffman, FAO-56 Ks, FAO-29 LR); when to leach; Mediterranean winter-rain leaching. Relevance: Crete (greenhouse vegetables, olive, citrus).

Source note: the page `fao.org/4/Y4263E/...` is FAO Irrigation & Drainage Paper 61 (Tanji & Kielen 2002, "Agricultural drainage water management in arid and semi-arid areas"); its Annex reproduces the Maas–Grattan-style tolerance table. `fao.org/4/t0234e/...` is FAO I&D Paper 29 Rev.1 (Ayers & Westcot). `fao.org/4/x0490e/...` is FAO I&D Paper 56 (Allen et al. 1998).

## Q1. How do salts accumulate under drippers, and what does it mean for 2 sensors at 2 depths under one dripper?

### Takeaway
Under surface drip the soil right below/around the emitter is a leached "low-salt bulb" whose salinity approximately reflects the irrigation water EC; salt piles up at the periphery of the wetted bulb, at the surface between emitters/lines, midway between laterals, and (for subsurface drip) above the buried line. Two sensors stacked under one dripper therefore sample the *best-leached* part of the profile: they tell you whether the leaching around the emitter is working (and track ECw/fertigation EC), but they systematically under-report the salt stock at the bulb edge and at the surface between emitters, which becomes dangerous when light rain moves it back into the root zone.

### Cited Findings
- Drip advantages under salinity: "soil in the wetted area around emitters is mostly leached of salts, and that is often where root density is the highest, particularly for row crops"; high-frequency drip "can maintain a relatively constant soil water content and soil salinity level over time near the drip lines." Disadvantage: "salt accumulates near the periphery of the wetted area", a concern when emitter placement does not coincide with the plant row; "Salt accumulation above buried drip lines also is a concern." — [Hanson & May 2011, UC ANR Pub. 8447 "Drip Irrigation Salinity Management for Row Crops"](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- "Near the drip emitter, soil salinity will be the least and will reflect the salinity of the irrigation water. Soil salinity increases with distance from the emitter so that relatively large values can be detected near the periphery of the wetted pattern." — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- Commercial fields irrigated with ECi 1.5–2.0 dS/m: low salinity extending downward from surface drip lines, larger salt accumulation between drip lines and near bed edges (sandy loam and clay loam); the low-salinity zone extended further horizontally in clay loam than sandy loam. Subsurface drip with ECi 2.5 dS/m: salt accumulated above the drip line with highest levels near the surface; lower around and below the line (figure classes up to ">10 dS/m"). — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- "Localized leaching": leaching is concentrated near the drip lines; "Soil salinity is the lowest in this highly leached zone and is a fairly accurate reflection of the irrigation water's own salinity. Leaching decreases with distance from drip lines, with no leaching midway between adjacent drip lines. There, the soil salinity is the highest." If applied volume is too small, salt can accumulate near the drip lines themselves; more applied water enlarges the low-salt zone. — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- The highly leached zone may be relatively small in sandy soils compared with fine-textured soils. — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- Under subsurface drip "no leaching occurs above the buried drip lines"; periodic leaching by rainfall or sprinkler is necessary, and salts must be moved below the drip line or they re-accumulate above it. — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- Hanson, May & Bendixen: "Low soil salinity occurs near the emitter... Zones of low salinity also extend downward beneath the drip lines... Salinity increases with depth and distance from the emitter. Midway between the drip lines, soil salinity near the soil surface is very high because little or no leaching occurs." With LF 5% vs 25% (Hoffman et al. 1985), the larger LF gives a larger low-salinity zone. Without leaching, salinity can be highest *near* the drip line and decrease with distance/depth (observed in April), with the opposite pattern once leaching occurred (July). — [Hanson, May & Bendixen 2003, "Drip Irrigation in Salt Affected Soil", Irrigation Association](https://mail.irrigationtoolbox.com/ReferenceDocuments/TechnicalPapers/IA/2003/IA03-0414.pdf)
- Field values (processing tomato, San Joaquin Valley): Site BR, ECw 0.34 dS/m, shallow saline groundwater: ECe 3–4 dS/m near the drip line but 7–10 dS/m elsewhere in the profile. Site DI (water table ~6 ft): ECe <~2 dS/m everywhere. A severe spring rainstorm with ponding leached the top ~1 ft at site DE. — [Hanson, May & Bendixen 2003](https://mail.irrigationtoolbox.com/ReferenceDocuments/TechnicalPapers/IA/2003/IA03-0414.pdf)
- FAO-29: with localized irrigation, salts accumulate "at the soil surface between emitters and at the outside edges of the area wetted"; danger arises when "a light rain moves the accumulated salt back into the root zone", greatest just after the first rains; recommends regular irrigations continue during a rain or until 50–100 mm has fallen. Localized systems apply water daily or near-daily at 2–8 L/h per emitter. — [FAO I&D 29 Rev.1, §2.4.5](https://www.fao.org/4/t0234e/T0234E03.htm)
- High-sulfate water field study: soluble salts accumulated near the surface 20–40 cm from the emitter during the season and decreased after winter rains (search-result summary of the record; full text not read). — [AGRIS record "Field salinity profile development under drip irrigation with high-sulfate water"](https://agris.fao.org/search/ar/records/65de57240f3e94b9e5cdd32e)
- Modelling (HYDRUS-2D, subsurface emitter): salinity decreases with distance from the emitter; irrigation duration/volume controls solute spreading. A tomato HYDRUS-2D study found top-soil salinity rises with irrigation-water salinity and surface salinity increases with larger emitter spacing and deeper emitters (simulation, one soil). — [Journal of Engineering, Univ. Baghdad 2020](https://www.joe.uobaghdad.edu.iq/index.php/main/article/view/1163); [search summary, academia.edu SelimT](https://independent.academia.edu/SelimT)
- Desalination zone centred on the emitter grows deeper with continued irrigation. — [Frontiers in Environmental Science 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2023.1130455/pdf)

### Inferences
- Two sensors directly under one dripper (e.g., shallow ~10–20 cm and deeper ~30–40 cm) sit inside the leached bulb. In steady, well-leached operation both should read close to a value set by ECw/fertigation EC × a modest concentration factor; a rising trend at the *shallow* sensor relative to the deep one suggests evaporative/upward accumulation or under-irrigation; a rising *deep* sensor with a stable shallow one suggests salts being pushed down (leaching working) or a rising saline water table.
- Because salinity near the emitter is the minimum of the profile, an alert threshold set on these sensors is a lower bound on root-zone salinity, not an average; in sandy soils (small bulb) the gap between "under emitter" and "bulb edge" is larger.
- Inverted profiles (shallow > deep, or near-emitter high) are diagnostic of insufficient applied volume (cf. Hanson's "April" pattern), so the *shape* of the 2-depth profile is informative, not only its level.
- The surface/periphery salt stock invisible to the sensors is the main risk at the first autumn rains in Crete; a rule keyed only to sensor readings will miss it — hence the FAO-29 "keep irrigating until 50–100 mm has fallen" rule is worth encoding as a calendar/rain-driven rule, independent of the sensors.
- Positional caveat: if laterals are offset from plant rows, or trees have multiple emitters, the sensor under one dripper may not represent where roots are.

### Gaps
- No quantitative ratio (bulb-edge ECe / under-emitter ECe) was found for typical Cretan soils/emitter spacings; figures in UC ANR 8447 show classes from <1.5 to >10 dS/m but are California-specific.
- No published sensor-placement standard (distance/depth from emitter) for continuous EC sensors was found; the only explicit sampling guidance found is UC's "within the first 6 inches (~15 cm) from the drip line" for soil cores (see Q2).

## Q2. How is root-zone salinity aggregated (uptake weighting, 40-30-20-10, worst-layer vs average)? Do FAO-56 / UC models use root-zone average ECe?

### Takeaway
All standard yield-response rules (Maas–Hoffman, FAO-29, FAO-56 Ks, FAO-61 tables) are defined on the **average root-zone ECe (saturated paste)**. FAO-29 / FAO-56 derive that average under a steady-state 40-30-20-10 % water-extraction pattern by quarters of the root zone (FAO-29's field procedure: sample four quarter-depths and average). The UC workgroup (Letey et al. 2011) and Corwin et al. 2007 conclude these steady-state guidelines overestimate LR and salinity damage, especially at low LF, and the newer UC guidelines use a **water-uptake-weighted** average root-zone ECe under high-frequency irrigation. For drip specifically, UC extension explicitly says the "true" root-zone average is unknowable from the spatial pattern and recommends comparing near-emitter samples (where roots are) against the crop threshold — i.e., a "where the roots are" reading rather than a full-profile average or a worst-layer reading.

### Cited Findings
- FAO-56: Ks under salinity applies "the average ECe for the root zone"; ECe defined as the EC of the soil solution brought to saturation (saturated paste extract), dS/m. — [FAO I&D 56, Ch. 8](https://www.fao.org/4/x0490e/x0490e0e.htm)
- FAO-56 Eq. 93: steady-state ECe estimated from ECiw and LF using the FAO-29 relation LR = ECiw/(5·ECe − ECiw) with LF in place of LR; "Equation 93 predicts ECe = 1.5 ECiw under conditions where a 15-20 percent leaching fraction is employed"; assumes a "40-30-20-10 percent water extraction pattern" upper-to-lower quarters; valid only if water quality and LF are constant over the season; local calibration recommended under winter-rain conditions. — [FAO I&D 56, Ch. 8](https://www.fao.org/4/x0490e/x0490e0e.htm)
- FAO-29 Table 4 footnote: ECe = 1.5 ECw "assumes a 15–20 percent leaching fraction and a 40-30-20-10 percent water use pattern for the upper to lower quarters of the root zone." Field procedure §2.4.2(iii): sample ECe in the four quarter-depths and "calculate the average root zone salinity", compare with Table 4. — [FAO I&D 29 Rev.1](https://www.fao.org/4/t0234e/T0234E03.htm)
- Letey (2005 critique of FAO-29 use by CA State Water Board): Ayers & Westcot assumed root uptake 40, 30, 20, 10 % in first–fourth quarters; analysis assumes continuous steady flow and constant soil-solution concentration at each point, which "do not accurately represent the field condition"; FAO-29 averages node concentrations via simple mass balance without dissolution/precipitation. — [Letey, DWR exhibit (waterboards.ca.gov)](https://waterboards.ca.gov/waterrights/water_issues/programs/bay_delta/sds_srjf/sds/docs/dwr010807_dwr22.pdf)
- UC workgroup: existing steady-state guidelines "overestimate the leaching requirement and the negative consequences of irrigating with saline waters"; the error is "particularly large at low leaching fractions" — described as fortuitous since low LF saves water. Authors: Letey, Hoffman, Hopmans, Grattan, Suarez, Corwin, Oster, Wu, Amrhein. — [Letey et al. 2011, Agric. Water Manage. 98:502–506 (IDEAS abstract)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/agiwat/v98y2011i4p502-506.html)
- A SSSA 2010 presentation abstract states the UC2 guidelines are "based on high-frequency irrigation and a water-uptake weighted average root zone ECe" (search snippet). — [ASA-CSSA-SSSA 2010 meeting abstract](https://scisoc.confex.com/crops/2010am/recordingredirect.cgi/id/9781)
- Corwin, Rhoades & Šimůnek 2007 compared steady-state (traditional LR, WATSUIT, water-production-function) vs transient models (TETrans, UNSATCHEM); transient models generally predict soil salinity more precisely but need hard-to-obtain data. — [Corwin et al. 2007, Agric. Water Manage. 90(3):165–180, doi 10.1016/j.agwat.2007.02.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agwat.2007.02.007) *(bibliographic details and model list from search results; full text not read)*
- UC on drip: comparing drip vs non-drip yield–salinity data is complicated because "soil salinity varies around drip lines"; researchers "used average soil salinity values over time near drip emitters for the root zone salinity levels." — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- UC note on drip: "The threshold ECe value is the maximum average root zone ECe at which no yield reduction should occur (Maas, 1990). The actual root zone salinity under drip irrigation at these sites is unknown because of spatially varying patterns of soil salinity, soil moisture, and probably root density around drip lines. The threshold value is provided as a reference only." — [Hanson, May & Bendixen 2003](https://mail.irrigationtoolbox.com/ReferenceDocuments/TechnicalPapers/IA/2003/IA03-0414.pdf)
- UC monitoring rule for drip row crops: collect samples near drip lines, determine ECe, compare to crop threshold; values above threshold "may indicate insufficient leaching"; for drip lines coinciding with rows, sample "within the first 6 inches from the drip line because research has shown that most of the roots will be found near the drip lines." Classic LF-from-ECw/average-ECe equations "were developed for flood and sprinkle irrigation" and "may not yield results that are useful" for drip. — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- 1:5 or 1:10 extract EC values "cannot be used with the values listed in Table 1" (thresholds are ECe-based); they can be used for trends. — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- Hoffman/UC: ECe thresholds may be 1–3 dS/m higher on gypsiferous soils. — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)

### Inferences
- With only 2 depths, an FAO-style 4-quarter average cannot be reconstructed; a defensible approximation is an uptake-weighted mean using the 40-30-20-10 pattern collapsed to two halves (upper half ≈ 70 %, lower half ≈ 30 %), if the two sensors are at roughly the upper-half and lower-half mid-depths of the current root zone. This is consistent with the FAO-29/56 basis but inherits its steady-state assumptions; under drip, the near-emitter location already biases low (Q1), so a 70/30 weighted value should be treated as a "near-emitter root-zone index", not the field ECe.
- A worst-layer (max) rule is more conservative and appropriate for *alerts* (early warning), while the weighted mean is the input consistent with the published yield functions (Maas–Hoffman, Ks). The literature found uses averages for yield prediction; no standard was found that prescribes worst-layer for yield.
- Given Letey et al. 2011, applying steady-state FAO-29 numbers to a high-frequency drip system errs on the safe side (overestimates damage/LR), so false alarms are more likely than missed ones — for the core-salinity alerts this is an acceptable bias, but should be stated.
- Sensor values are typically bulk EC or pore-water EC, not ECe; any comparison with Maas–Hoffman thresholds requires conversion to ECe-equivalent (other notes cover conversion); UC explicitly warns against mixing extract types.

### Gaps
- No quantitative error estimate (e.g., ±dS/m) was found for "uptake-weighted vs linear average vs worst-layer" under drip; Letey et al. 2011 quantifies overestimation qualitatively in the abstract only (full text paywalled; UNL copy returned 403).
- The exact UC2 weighting function (exponential uptake vs 40-30-20-10) was not retrieved.
- Unverified (from background knowledge, not confirmed this session): FAO-29's alternative high-frequency LR form LR = ECw/(2·max ECe) and the common rule ECsw (soil water at field capacity) ≈ 2·ECe; a targeted search did not find a source for the "2·max ECe" form.

## Q3. How do crop stage and root-depth evolution affect which depth matters?

### Takeaway
Standard rules define the "root zone" as the current effective rooting depth (FAO-56 Zr grows with stage), and under drip most roots of row crops concentrate in the top ~40 cm near the line. Early in the season (transplant/seedling) only the shallow layer matters and is also the layer most exposed to surface salt; later the deeper layer joins the active zone. Literature found gives root-distribution figures but no explicit stage-dependent salinity weighting rule.

### Cited Findings
- Drip row crops: roots "highly concentrated near drip lines (the zone of wettest soil) if the drip line placement coincides with the plant row" (Hanson & May 2007); offset drip lines shift highest root density away from the leached zone, sometimes "putting most of the roots in areas of higher soil salinity." — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- Trickle-irrigated tomato (Tunisia): ~40 % of roots between 10 and 40 cm depth, where the highest soil-water depletion was recorded. Portuguese SDI tomato: most roots in top 40 cm, root-length density 0.5–1.4 cm cm⁻³. — [search summaries: Lund Univ. record](https://www.lu.se/lup/publication/69d2e997-3c61-4196-8a40-f2dd59354735); [Univ. Évora, "Tomato root distribution... under different subsurface drip irrigation regimes and depths"](https://dspace.uevora.pt/rdpc/bitstream/10174/1875/1/fulltext.pdf)
- Subsurface drip: periodic sprinkler leaching of salt above buried lines "will be necessary for stand establishment if winter and spring rainfall levels are too low" — i.e., the shallow layer is critical at establishment. — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- Salt-tolerance data are partly stage-specific: FAO-29 Table 4 notes grape and grapefruit tolerance based on tree growth, not yield; FAO-61 olive rating based on "seedling growth and fruit yield". — [FAO I&D 29](https://www.fao.org/4/t0234e/T0234E03.htm); [FAO I&D 61 Annex](https://www.fao.org/4/Y4263E/y4263e0e.htm)
- FAO-56 Ks salinity formulation assumes RAW/p unchanged by salinity ("may or may not be a good assumption for some crops"); Eqs. 91–92 are "only approximate estimates" over weeks/months, not daily; restrict use to ECe < ECthreshold + 50/b. — [FAO I&D 56, Ch. 8](https://www.fao.org/4/x0490e/x0490e0e.htm)

### Inferences
- A practical rule: weight the two sensors by the fraction of the current root depth they represent. At establishment (Zr shallow, e.g. ≤20–25 cm), use the shallow sensor alone (or worst of the two if the deep one is within Zr); as Zr reaches the deep sensor, move toward the 70/30 weighting.
- For trees (olive, citrus) with permanent, deeper root systems, both depths are always in the root zone; the near-emitter location matters more than stage.
- Because FAO-56 Ks is meant for weekly-to-monthly averages, any Ks-based irrigation adjustment should use smoothed (multi-day) salinity, not instantaneous sensor readings.

### Gaps
- No source found that prescribes stage-specific depth weighting for salinity alerts; no stage-specific Maas–Hoffman thresholds were retrieved for tomato/vegetables (germination/seedling sensitivity data exist in the literature but were not retrieved).

## Q4. Standard decision rules: Maas–Hoffman, FAO-56 Ks, FAO-29 LR, leaching triggers, Mediterranean winter rain; thresholds for Cretan crops

### Takeaway
Yield rule: Yr(%) = 100 − b·(ECe − a) for ECe > a (Maas–Hoffman), applied to average root-zone ECe. FAO-56 converts this to an ET stress factor Ks through Ky. Leaching requirement (FAO-29, Rhoades): LR = ECw / (5·ECe_t − ECw); ECe ≈ 1.5·ECw at LF 15–20 %. Leaching need not be continuous: FAO-29 says timing is not critical so long as tolerance is not exceeded for extended periods; under drip UC sets the trigger as "near-emitter ECe above crop threshold" and recommends applying at least ETc (giving localized LF ≈ 10–25 %). For Mediterranean climates: heavy late-autumn/early-winter irrigation to refill the profile so winter rain completes leaching; keep irrigating during the first rains until 50–100 mm has fallen.

### Cited Findings
**Maas–Hoffman / thresholds**
- Yr = 100 − b(ECe − a), a = threshold (dS/m), b = slope (%/dS/m), ECe = mean saturated-paste EC of the root zone. — [FAO I&D 61 Annex](https://www.fao.org/4/Y4263E/y4263e0e.htm)
- FAO-61 table values (a / b / rating): Tomato 2.5 / 9.9 / MS; Pepper 1.5 / 14 / MS; Cucumber 2.5 / 13 / MS; Lettuce 1.3 / 13 / MS; Muskmelon (fruit yield) 1.0 / 8.4 / MS; Grape 1.5 / 9.6 / MS; Orange 1.3 / 13.1 / S; Lemon 1.5 / 12.8 / S; Olive: no numeric value, rated MT (seedling growth and fruit yield). — [FAO I&D 61 Annex](https://www.fao.org/4/Y4263E/y4263e0e.htm)
- FAO-29 Table 4 (ECe / ECw in dS/m at 100 % / 90 % / 75 % / 50 % yield / 0 %): Tomato 2.5/1.7, 3.5/2.3, 5.0/3.4, 7.6/5.0, 13/8.4; Pepper 1.5/1.0, 2.2/1.5, 3.3/2.2, 5.1/3.4, 8.6/5.8; Cucumber 2.5/1.7, 3.3/2.2, 4.4/2.9, 6.3/4.2, 10/6.8; Lettuce 1.3/0.9, 2.1/1.4, 3.2/2.1, 5.1/3.4, 9.0/6.0; Potato 1.7/1.1 … 10/6.7; Squash-zucchini 4.7/3.1, 5.8/3.8, 7.4/4.9, 10/6.7, 15/10; Spinach 2.0/1.3 … 15/10; Strawberry 1.0/0.7, 1.3/0.9, 1.8/1.2, 2.5/1.7, 4/2.7; Orange 1.7/1.1, 2.3/1.6, 3.3/2.2, 4.8/3.2, 8.0/5.3; Grapefruit 1.8/1.2 … 8.0/5.4 (growth-based); Grape 1.5/1.0, 2.5/1.7, 4.1/2.7, 6.7/4.5, 12/7.9 (growth-based). Olive, lemon, avocado, melon, eggplant not in Table 4 (qualitative only). Note FAO-29 orange threshold (1.7) differs from FAO-61/Maas–Grattan (1.3). — [FAO I&D 29 Rev.1, Table 4](https://www.fao.org/4/t0234e/T0234E03.htm); [FAO I&D 61](https://www.fao.org/4/Y4263E/y4263e0e.htm)
- Olive (Arbequina, field, Spain/Aragón): vegetative salinity tolerance ECe threshold = 4 dS/m, slope = −12 %/dS/m; dead trees mostly where ECe > 10 dS/m; Maas & Hoffman (1977) class olive MT (ECe 3–6 dS/m). — [Aragüés et al., CITA "vegetative" paper](https://citarea.cita-aragon.es/bitstream/10532/2572/1/Aragues_vegetative.pdf)
- Olive (Picual, 8 years drip with NaCl/CaCl₂ water, Spain): Ca supply, drip until winter rest, seasonal Mediterranean rainfall leaching and a tolerant cultivar allowed water up to 10 dS/m long-term without affecting growth and yield. — [Melgar et al. 2009, Agric. Water Manage. 96:1105 (IDEAS)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/agiwat/v96y2009i7p1105-1113.html); [ISHS Acta Hort. 949_40](https://ishs.org/ishs-article/949_40/)
- Olive (Israel, Negev, 12 cultivars, drip): ECw 4.2 dS/m vs 1.2 dS/m showed "only a low rate of retardation"; cultivars differed significantly in yield. — [HortScience 43(2):320](https://journals.ashs.org/view/journals/hortsci/43/2/article-p320.xml)
- Greenhouse/soilless (Sonneveld & van der Burg 1991, recirculating): thresholds for tomato, cucumber, sweet pepper 2.3–3.5 dS/m; yield decrease 2.3–7.6 %/dS/m (root-environment EC, not ECe). — [NJAS, Wageningen](https://library.wur.nl/ojs/index.php/njas/article/download/16546/15960)
- Rockwool recirculation practice (Italy): discharge when EC exceeded 4.5, 6.5 or 7.5 dS/m (experiment-dependent) and/or NO₃ < 1.0 mol/m³. — [ARPI Univ. Pisa record](https://arpi.unipi.it/handle/11568/188758)
- Drip vs historical data: UC review "mixed": lettuce, onion, potato less affected under drip than predicted; tomato, maize, bell pepper responded as predicted. Varietal spread large: 10 processing tomato cultivars drip-irrigated with 6.0 dS/m water gave 35–73 % relative yield. — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- Tomato under drip (Pasternak et al. 1986): ECw 4.5 dS/m → ~10–12 % yield loss vs 1.2 dS/m; ECw 7.5 → ~60 % loss. — [Hanson, May & Bendixen 2003](https://mail.irrigationtoolbox.com/ReferenceDocuments/TechnicalPapers/IA/2003/IA03-0414.pdf)

**FAO-56 Ks**
- Eq. 89 (yield–salinity, FAO-29), Eq. 90 (yield–ET via Ky, FAO-33); Eq. 91 Ks (salinity only, Dr < RAW) and Eq. 92 (salinity + water stress, Dr > RAW), both for ECe > ECe_threshold. Seasonal Ky ≈ 1 for many crops; Table 24: tomato 1.05, maize 1.25, onion 1.1, beans 1.15, cotton 0.85. Salinity and water stress treated as additive. Use only for ECe < ECthreshold + 50/b; Ks reduction should be applied with the dual-Kc approach under high-frequency irrigation. Equations appear as images; the commonly cited combined form (not verified as text this session) is Ks = 1 − [b/(100·Ky)]·(ECe − ECe_threshold). — [FAO I&D 56, Ch. 8](https://www.fao.org/4/x0490e/x0490e0e.htm)
- Validation: FAO-56 Ks method underestimated ET by ~20 % at ECe ≈ 6 dS/m (potato/broad bean lysimeters). — [Agric. Water Manage. 98(4):588–596 (IDEAS)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/agiwat/v98y2011i4p588-596.html)
- Worked example (teaching, Lisbon): threshold 1.0, b = 19 %/dS/m, ECe = 1.5 → Ks ≈ 0.92 (illustrative). — [ISA Lisbon Kd.pdf](https://fenix.isa.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/563022967888430/Kd.pdf)

**Leaching requirement & triggers**
- LR (%) = ECw × 100 / (5·ECe − ECw), ECe = crop threshold (UC/ANR formulation of Rhoades). — [UC Davis soil health / ANR](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/229/224); USGS ModelMuse also lists "RHOADES Equation ==> ECw/(5*ECe − ECw)". — [USGS ModelMuse help](https://water.usgs.gov/nrp/gwsoftware/ModelMuse/Help/crop-leaching-requirement-pane.html)
- Variant in literature: LR = ECiw/[5·(ECe* − ECiw)] with ECe* usually the threshold (Maas 1990) — note the bracket differs from the FAO form; check before use. — [Dudley, "Special submissions" (biosaline.org)](https://halophyteskh.biosaline.org/sites/default/files/content/MarginalResources_MWater/LynnMDudley.pdf)
- NMSU: separate LF equations for high-frequency (drip, sprinkler) systems: LF = 0.1794/(ECe/ECw)^3.0417; LF larger for low-frequency systems. — [NMSU Guide W-102](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_w/W102/)
- FAO-29 timing: "The timing of leachings does not appear to be critical provided crop tolerance is not exceeded" for extended periods; leaching not needed until accumulating salinity is expected to exceed crop tolerance. — [FAO I&D 29 §2.4.2(ii)](https://www.fao.org/4/t0234e/T0234E03.htm)
- UC drip: maintenance leaching aims to keep "soil salinity near drip lines from exceeding the threshold"; irrigation 2–3×/week may suffice for at least moderately sensitive crops; daily for salt-sensitive crops. Apply at least ETc: ≈ETc gives LF ≈ 10–25 %, mostly around/beneath the line. Localized LF simulated at 7.7 / 24.5 / 30.5 % for 60 / 100 / 115 % ETc (tomato); almonds (Nightingale 1991) measured LF 4–6 % at 50 % ETc, 10–22 % at 100 %, 31–36 % at 150 % — water-balance method underestimates drip leaching. Optimal LF may need trial-and-error. Reclamation: twice-weekly larger applications reclaimed faster than daily small ones (Hanson, Hopmans & Šimůnek 2008). — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- Raising LF under drip raised yields of celery, cauliflower, lettuce, cowpea, tomato (Hoffman et al. 1979; Hoffman & Jobes 1983; Jobes et al. 1981). — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)

**Mediterranean winter rain**
- FAO-29: where winter rains may not provide all necessary leaching, a heavy late-autumn/early-winter irrigation refills the profile so winter rains complete leaching with low-salt water; with drip, keep irrigating during the first rain or until 50–100 mm has fallen (light rain moves surface/periphery salt into the root zone). — [FAO I&D 29 §2.4.5–2.4.6](https://www.fao.org/4/t0234e/T0234E03.htm)
- FAO-56: steady-state ECe estimate (Eq. 93) needs local calibration under winter-rain conditions. — [FAO I&D 56](https://www.fao.org/4/x0490e/x0490e0e.htm)
- Ebro basin, Spain (drip grapevine, peach, nectarine 2007–2012): ECe rose in most irrigation seasons and fell in most non-irrigation seasons; no multi-year salinization trend because of annual accumulation/leaching cycles; larger ECe increases under more severe deficit irrigation; ΔECe linearly and negatively correlated with LF (P < 0.01). Table grape: LF 0.20 (Autumn Royal) and 0.13 (Crimson) led to high salinity/sodicity. — [Aragüés et al. 2014/15, Irrig. Sci. 33, doi 10.1007/s00271-014-0449-x (CITA record)](https://citarea.cita-aragon.es/handle/10532/2667?locale=en)
- Crete (Chania olive, WOCAT): saline water can be applied in larger amounts at spots to leach salts deeper; considerable reduction of autumn/winter rainfall has caused water scarcity (implying less natural leaching). — [WOCAT QCAT 4626, Kosmas](https://qcat.wocat.net/lo/summary/4626/?as=html)

**ECw guideline classes**
- FAO-29 degree of restriction (salinity, ECw): none < 0.7 dS/m; slight-to-moderate 0.7–3.0; severe > 3.0 (as summarized from the page text). — [FAO I&D 29](https://www.fao.org/4/t0234e/T0234E03.htm)
- UC: deciduous salt-sensitive trees, ECw > 3.2 dS/m "severe". — [UC ANR (ccvegcrops)](https://ucanr.edu/sites/ccvegcrops/files/237124.pdf)

### Inferences
- Alert ladder consistent with the literature (sensor value converted to ECe-equivalent, near-emitter, uptake-weighted): (i) "watch" at ECe ≥ a (threshold); (ii) "act/leach" when ECe stays ≥ a for an extended period (FAO-29's "not exceeded for extended periods" — a multi-day window, not single readings); (iii) "yield loss estimate" = b·(ECe − a) % reported only within a < ECe < a + 50/b (FAO-56 validity range).
- Numeric examples: tomato a = 2.5, b = 9.9 → 10 % loss at ECe 3.5, 25 % at ~5.0; pepper a = 1.5, b = 14 → 10 % loss at ~2.2; orange a = 1.3 (or 1.7 FAO-29), b = 13.1; olive a ≈ 4 (vegetative, Arbequina), b ≈ 12.
- LR examples (FAO formula, threshold-based): tomato with ECw 1.0 → LR = 1/(12.5 − 1) ≈ 8.7 %; pepper with ECw 1.0 → 1/(7.5 − 1) ≈ 15 %; orange (a = 1.3) with ECw 1.0 → 1/(6.5 − 1) ≈ 18 %. Per Letey et al. 2011 these are upper-bound estimates for high-frequency drip.
- For Crete, the natural annual cycle (accumulation Apr–Oct, leaching by winter rain) means a seasonal rising trend in summer is expected and not by itself a trigger; the relevant checks are (a) whether winter brings sensor EC back to baseline (if not → reclamation leaching before season) and (b) the first-rain risk window.
- Fertigated greenhouse soils (soil-grown, as in Crete/Almería) are not substrate systems; Sonneveld thresholds (root-environment EC 2.3–3.5) are in a different EC basis than ECe and must not be mixed with Maas–Hoffman ECe thresholds.

## Q5. Role of irrigation water EC (ECw) and fertigation EC in judging accumulation; soil-solution EC / fertigation EC ratio as an indicator

### Takeaway
Under drip the near-emitter soil salinity "reflects the salinity of the irrigation water"; at steady state with LF 15–20 %, ECe ≈ 1.5·ECw (FAO-29/56). So the ratio of measured root-zone salinity to the EC of the applied water (including fertilizer salts during fertigation) is a direct, physically grounded accumulation indicator: a ratio near the expected steady-state concentration factor means leaching is adequate; a ratio rising over time means accumulation (LF too low). No extension standard publishing a target soil-solution/fertigation-EC ratio was found.

### Cited Findings
- "The electrical conductivity of the low-salt soil near drip lines will reflect the electrical conductivity of the irrigation water"; the salinity of the low-salt zone depends largely on irrigation water salinity, while its *extent* depends on LF. — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224); [Hanson, May & Bendixen 2003](https://mail.irrigationtoolbox.com/ReferenceDocuments/TechnicalPapers/IA/2003/IA03-0414.pdf)
- ECe = 1.5·ECw at LF 15–20 % with 40-30-20-10 uptake. — [FAO I&D 29](https://www.fao.org/4/t0234e/T0234E03.htm); [FAO I&D 56](https://www.fao.org/4/x0490e/x0490e0e.htm)
- LF estimation from ECw and average root-zone ECe (or chloride) is a classic method but was developed for flood/sprinkler; may not be useful for drip because salt and root patterns vary horizontally and vertically. — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224)
- Higher-salinity water (ECi 1.1 vs 0.3 dS/m) gave higher soil salinity near the drip line; at site DE the higher near-line values "appear to reflect the differences in the irrigation water electrical conductivity". — [UC ANR 8447](https://soilhealth.ucdavis.edu/download_file/view/238/224); [Hanson, May & Bendixen 2003](https://mail.irrigationtoolbox.com/ReferenceDocuments/TechnicalPapers/IA/2003/IA03-0414.pdf)
- Mediterranean greenhouse tomato: soil-solution EC from zero-tension lysimeters generally lower than from suction cups at all depths; suction cups sample pores with longer residence times. EC of resident soil solution usually higher than wetting-front EC (incomplete mixing). Almería-area study: suction-cup solution "very different" from the irrigation solution, ion concentrations tracking tensiometric potential. Greenhouse study: EC, Mg²⁺, Na⁺, Cl⁻ significantly higher at 0.25 m than at 0.10 m; a linear model estimating soil-solution composition from soil moisture and prior composition was proposed for fertigation design. — [search-result summaries: IDEAS AWM 178:171–179 (2016)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/agiwat/v178y2016icp171-179.html); [Commun. Soil Sci. Plant Anal. CSS-120018980](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1081/CSS-120018980) *(abstract-level; full texts not read)*
- Substrate drain-EC control: computer-controlled leaching kept drain EC 2.9–3.5 dS/m and cut effluent by 54 %; under cloudy conditions drain EC falls (low transpiration, high LF). — [Scielo Brazil, sand-substrate tomato](https://www.scielo.br/j/asagr/a/Tfpv5wdGzjyWCkPYnPGX4hQ/abstract/?lang=en)

### Inferences
- A useful accumulation indicator: CF = EC_soil(ECe-equivalent, near-emitter, weighted) / ECw_applied, where ECw_applied is the volume-weighted EC of everything delivered (water + fertilizer injection). Steady-state reference CF ≈ 1.5 (FAO, LF 15–20 %); FAO-29 relation implies roughly CF ≈ (1 + LF)/(5·LF) → LF 10 % ≈ 2.2, LF 30 % ≈ 0.87 (derived from LR = ECw/(5ECe − ECw); inference, not a published table). A sustained CF climb → insufficient leaching; CF ≪ 1 after rain → rain dilution.
- During fertigation pulses, raw sensor EC rises because of fertilizer salts that are nutrients, not "salinity damage" in the Maas–Hoffman sense; alerts should use the CF against applied-solution EC (or chloride/sodium if available) rather than absolute EC alone, otherwise fertigated greenhouses will produce false salinity alarms.
- Suction-cup vs lysimeter vs ECe differences (FERTINNOWA open question) mean any pore-water EC sensor needs crop thresholds re-expressed on its own basis; the ×2 pore-water/ECe rule (background knowledge, unverified here) is the usual bridge.

- No source found separating fertilizer-salt EC from "damaging" salinity in soil-sensor alert logic.
